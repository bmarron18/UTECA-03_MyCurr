%%% Major Themes %%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
1. Mother-Daughter Relationships
The novel explores the complex relationships between Chinese-American immigrant mothers and their American-born daughters. It delves into the expectations and cultural differences that create tension and misunderstanding between the generations.The mothers’ traditional values and expectations often clash with their daughters' desires for independence and assimilation. 

2. Cultural Identity and Assimilation
The theme of cultural identity is central to the novel, focusing on the immigrant experience and the challenge of maintaining one’s heritage while assimilating into a new culture. There is conflict between traditional Chinese values and American culture and the characters struggle to reconcile their cultural identity with their desire to fit into American society.

3. Generational Conflict
The novel portrays the generational conflicts between immigrant parents and their children, highlighting the differences in values, expectations, and life experiences. There is a generational gap in understanding and communication and the generational differences affect family dynamics.

4. Self-Discovery and Empowerment
The characters’ journeys toward self-discovery and empowerment are central to the novel. Each woman grapples with her own identity, desires, and sense of agency. There is personal growth of each character as they confront their past and cultural expectations.
    
5. Family Legacy and Loyalty
The theme of family legacy and loyalty explores the impact of family history and heritage on individual lives and the ways in which the past influences the present and future.

6. Communication and Misunderstanding
The novel highlights the difficulties of communication and the misunderstandings that arise from cultural and generational differences. Language barriers affect relationships and the characters attempt to bridge gaps in understanding.

--- The Voice from the Wall: Lena St. Clair (Ch 6)
Growing up in Oakland, Lena seems to have a morbid fascnation with death after she learns her grandfather had a beggar killed. Lena's mom seems to add to this state of the macabre by instilling any number of fears in Lena. Eventually Lena begins to see with "her Chinese eys" and what she sees are scary portraits of everyday life. She becomes rather withdrawn but not as much her mom who, in Lena's eyes, has become a ghost. Lena's American dad is oblivious to these happenings and is excited to move the family to a new apatment in a wealthier part of town. The family is moving up in the world! In the new apartment Lena's mom become increasingly dissatified with the aprtment and its the furnishings and begins an endless phase of reorganizing. The new apartment is rather noisy both from street noises and from the neighbors, and Lena, who once had a comforting relation with the regularties of the sounds from the street, is moved to a new area where she hears the constant and ferocious fighting of the neighbors. Lena believes the neighbors are on the verge of killing each other each and every night. Meanwhile, Lena learns that her mom who was pregnant has lost the baby. Lena's mom is plunged into despair and loses her vitality day by day until she no longer gets out of bed and must be hospitalized. For Lena, her mom truly has become ghost. One night a loud knock at the door reveals the neighbor Teresa, a girl slightly older than Lena who explains her mom has kicked her out of the house. Lena is horrified but Teresa seems unconcerned and uses Lena's bedroom window to access the fire escape. Teresa explins that her plan is to disappear for a few hours and then sneak back into her room via the fire escape. Later that evening when Lena hears Teresa and her mom engaged in a confrontation of tears and hugs, Lena realizes that these people actually love each other and this gives Lena the courage to begin pulling her mom back to life.

--- Rules of the Game: Waverly Jong (Ch 5)
Waverly and her family live in Chinatown. The family has modest means but is well-fed and comfortable. As a result of a nearly-discarded chess set, a Christmas gift from the local church Santa, Waverly begins to learn chess. AT first her brothers must explain the moves and gladly accept Lifesavers for Waverly's losses. But one day Waverly meets Lau Po who shares his great chess secrets with her. She is intoxicated. Waverly rapidly advances to tournament play and becomes a national champion at nine years-old! The community, and especially her mom, is very proud of her. Her family makes many concessions so that Waverly can continue to study and play chess. Waverly seems to accept these concessions as matter-of-fact events perhaps not recognizing the sacrifices being made on her behalf. As Waverly's fame spreads, Waverly begins to feel that she is constantly being put up for exhibition by her mom. One day things come to a head. Waverly harshly states that if her mom wants to show off, she should learn to play chess herself. Waverly, filled with rage and shame, runs off for a few hours. When she returns, she finds that her mom simply ignores her, in essence revoking her special privileges. Initially hurt, Waverly recovers and plots her next move.

--- The Moon Lady: Ying-Ying St. Clair (Ch 4)
Ying-Ying is a rather privileged young girl. She is taken care of by her nanny, Amah, dresses in fine clothes, and is surrounded by a wealthy family that can afford to perform all of the important societal rituals. She even has younger step-sisters from her father's concubines. Ying-Ying and her family will participate in the (harvest) Moon Festival; an extravagant affair that includes an outing on a boat that "looked like a floating teahouse" and an evening of theater starring the Moon Lady. Ying-Ying is especially primped and groomed for the occasion by Amah who also gives her strict rules of behavior and the promise of a secret wish fulfilled awarded by the Moon Lady herself. To receive the Moon Lady's award Ying-Ying must behave perfectly and must not think of herself in her wish. On the boat Ying-Ying becomes fascinated with feast preparations and accidently gets blood on her new clothes. But to wash it off, she uses even more blood! The horrified Amah undresses her leaving Ying-Ying in her underwear at the back of the boat

--- The Red Candle: Lindo Jong (Ch 3)	-----
Lindo Jong comes from a median-income family in a small village. At two-years-old she is betrothed to a baby boy, Tyan-yu. As the years go by, Lindo realizes that her future husband is a spoiled rich kid and does not look forward to the marriage. Things become much worse when Lindo's family loses everything in a flood and young Lindo, then age 12, is forced to live with her future husband's family, the Huang. Tyan-yu's mother Huang Tattai treats Lindo as a servant and Lindo grows up with little freedom, incessant demands, and no self-esteem. On the wedding day Lindo has a revelation of self-realization and worth, and on the wedding night she realizes that Tyan-yu is really just a scared boy, and treats him like a younger brother. With no grandchildren forthcoming Huang Taitai becomes increasingly absurd in her attempts to get Lindo pregnant because Tyan-yu lies about their intimacy. Lindo finally hatches a plan that will "save face" for her family and free her from the Huang. Ultimately, she is freed from her obligations and given money to go to America.

--- Scar: An-Mei Hsu (Ch 2)	-----
An-Mei recounts her childhood. Her mother has left the children (An-Mei and her brother) to marry another man after their father's death. The children live in "Auntie's house" along with their grandmother, Popo. The family hates An-Mei's mom (she is a ghost), and Auntie is sharp tongued. At the deathbed of Popo "the ghost" appears and An-Mei recalls a deeply buried family tragedy: An-Mei's mom tried to take the children but Popo and Auntie intervened. In the scuffle, An-Mei is terribly burned, almost to death, by a caldron of boiling soup. In the end An-Mei comes to love and respect her mother.

--- The Joy Luck Club: Jing-Mei Woo (Ch 1)	-----
Chapter 1 introduces the formation of the Joy Luck Club, a gathering of four Chinese-American immigrant women who come together to play mahjong and share their experiences and hopes. The chapter is narrated from the perspective of June (Jing-mei) Woo, the daughter of one of the original members, Suyuan Woo. June reflects on her mother’s legacy and the club's significance. After her mother's death, June is asked to take her place in the club and ultimately, to tell the story of her mother to long-lost relatives: her step-sisters! This chapter sets up the central theme of generational and cultural conflicts and introduces the intertwining lives and stories of the club's members.
